Sphere Machine

At low RPM you see the object. At high RPM many complete orientations are superposed into one visible form. The same orientations are then used to estimate the probability that each point in space is occupied.

This extends the Infinite Camera idea in Spherism: not just “does the object look spherical?”, but “what spatial probability field is produced when orientation is averaged away?”

Next stage · probability → quantum mechanics

Now compare the classical sphere with quantum probability.

The left-hand simulation asks how often a point is occupied under many classical orientations. Quantum mechanics instead assigns a probability through the state itself. Use the hydrogen examples below to see where spherical symmetry survives, where nodes appear, and where directional structure returns.

Classical vs quantum radial structure

Same word “probability”; different physics.

For the rotating object, probability means the fraction of sampled orientations that occupy a point. For a pure quantum state, position probability is obtained from the Born rule, |ψ(x)|². The curves below are normalised for shape comparison, not placed on a shared physical scale.

classical: pS(x) = ⟨1B(R−1x)⟩R

quantum: pQ(x) = |ψ(x)|²   (pure state)

The 2p shell-average option is deliberately an incoherent equal mixture of the three m sublevels, not a coherent sum of amplitudes. Averaging the m sublevels removes the preferred direction and restores isotropic spatial density.

A) How the sphere is built

Average the object over rotations.

Let B be the object and 1B(x) its indicator: 1 inside the object and 0 outside. Define the rotational occupancy field:

PS(x) = ∫SO(3) 1B(R−1x) dμ(R)

Because the averaging measure over rotations is itself rotationally invariant, PS(Qx) = PS(x) for every rotation Q. Therefore the fully averaged field can depend only on radius:

PS(x) = p(r),   r = ‖x‖

That is the precise reason the rotational average becomes spherical: directional information is removed and radial structure remains.

B) What is borrowed from quantum mechanics

Probability and symmetry averaging — not quantum identity.

In Sphere Machine, p(x) is classical: it is the fraction of sampled orientations for which a spatial point lies inside the object.

p(x) = (# orientations occupying x) / (# orientations sampled)

For a quantum density operator ρ, position probability is:

PQ(x) = ⟨x|ρ|x⟩

A rotationally averaged quantum state can be written as:

ρ̄ = ∫SO(3) U(R)ρU(R)† dμ(R)

The bridge is therefore symmetry averaging → invariant probability structure. Genuine quantum superposition additionally contains complex amplitudes, relative phase and interference; the rotating-object simulation does not.

Why the 2p average becomes spherical

Angular information can disappear under an equal mixture.

ρ2p,mix = ⅓ Σm=−11 |2,1,m⟩⟨2,1,m|

Σm=−ll |Ylm(θ,φ)|² = (2l+1)/(4π)

The spherical-harmonic addition theorem makes the angular sum independent of direction. This is a particularly clean quantum analogue of the classical rotational-averaging idea: the individual 2p states can be directional, while an equal incoherent mixture across the full m multiplet has isotropic spatial density.

Hydrogen reference

1s and 2s are already spherically symmetric.

|ψ1s(r)|² ∝ e−2r/a₀

|ψ2s(r)|² ∝ (2 − r/a₀)²e−r/a₀

The 2s state adds an important lesson: spherical symmetry does not imply featureless probability. It contains a spherical radial node at r = 2a₀. The 2p states instead introduce angular nodes and directional structure.
