Rotational geometry laboratory

Sphere Machine

Take a cube, pyramid, torus, sphere, or an almost two-dimensional plate. Rotate it about the axes you choose. Watch what its outer rotational envelope becomes.

The object itself never stops being what it is. The experiment asks a different question: as its orientation changes, which directions can its outermost points occupy?

What is actually happening?

Rotation preserves radius.

For any point fixed in the object, rotation changes its direction from the centre but not its distance from the centre. Its possible positions therefore lie on a sphere of that radius.

The bright trace follows points at the object’s maximum radius. With only one rotational degree of freedom, those points usually draw circles. As the orientation programme explores more independent angular phases, those same fixed-radius points can occupy many more directions.

Important limitation

The cube never becomes a sphere.

Its instantaneous geometry remains a cube. Its mass distribution also remains cube-like. What can become spherical is the set or time-distribution of positions reached by selected points, and in particular the outer directional envelope under sufficiently rich orientation sampling.

This distinction matters. “Everything is basically a sphere” is most defensible here as a statement about rotational orbits, envelopes and symmetry, not about erasing the underlying geometry.

The compact mathematics

A rotation cannot change distance from the origin.

‖R(t)x‖² = xᵀR(t)ᵀR(t)x = xᵀx = ‖x‖²

So each material point travels on a spherical shell determined by its own radius. Whether the trace is merely a circle, a band, or becomes broadly sphere-filling depends on which orientations are actually sampled.

From proposition to experiment

This demonstrator was built to provide an interactive geometric test-bed alongside André Baumann’s Zenodo record 16985564. The demo deliberately separates the stronger mathematical statement about rotational orbits from the looser visual intuition that “everything is basically a sphere.”
